Labelling guideabout 5 minutes

How does this paper use this event?

Each paper page lists the gravitational-wave events the paper names. For each one, judge how this paper uses that event. That means its actual use, not the paper's topic, and not how it uses other events. Read the paper itself: the list of places where an event is named only tells you where to look.

Answer Q1–Q3 independently; tick every one that applies

Q1. Does the paper analyse the strain data for this event?Its own analysis of the detector data. The original discovery or catalogue analysis and any independent analysis count alike.
Q2. Does it use released samples for this event?Released posterior/PE samples, or sample sets conditioned on the event (e.g. EOS samples informed by GW170817), used in its own analysis. The samples must come from someone else or an earlier paper: a paper's own posteriors from analysing the strain are Q1, not Q2.
Q3. Does it use the event's published measurements with their uncertainties?Credible intervals, ranges or bounds for masses, spins, distance, sky position, H0, ... that the paper USES: as inputs to a calculation, figure or table, or to compare its own results against, even in the text (e.g. "exceeds the 1.61-2.52 solar mass primary of GW190425"). Merely quoting them as background (e.g. listing an H0 value among other measurements in the introduction) does not count. A released sky map or alert localisation counts here. The numbers must be published by someone else or an earlier paper: a paper reporting its own new measurements (e.g. a catalogue giving the masses it inferred) is Q1, not Q3.

Your own results are Q1. Q2 and Q3 are about products someone else (or an earlier paper) released or published. A catalogue reporting the masses it inferred is Q1, not Q3.

Only if Q1–Q3 are all "no"

Q4. If none of those: does it use anything the event showed?Its findings, implications or properties are discussed, or used to motivate, compare with, interpret or argue for the paper's own work; a central or rounded value ("~1.4 solar masses", "black holes of ~30 solar masses exist in binaries") counts. Read this broadly. If not -- the event is only named or cited as background, as in "gravitational waves have been observed [1]" -- it is cited_only.

If the answer is yes, the label is qualitative. If no, it is cited in passing.

Examples

A catalogue estimates each event's parameters from the dataQ1 for every event
Reweights "the GW170817-informed EOS samples"Q2
Builds Gaussians from a published chirp-mass interval (not the released samples)Q3, not Q2
"exceeds the 1.61–2.52 M☉ primary of GW190425", as a comparisonQ3
Chooses equations of state that satisfy GW170817's tidal limits, without restating the numbersQ3
Follow-up observations planned from the alert sky mapQ3
Lists GW170817's H0 ± error among other H0 measurements in its introductionQ4 (quoted as background)
"the merger of a ~23 M☉ BH and a ~2.6 M☉ object" (rounded values)Q4
"Formation scenarios proposed to explain GW150914" motivate the studyQ4
Excludes the event from an analysis because of a property (low significance, an outlier)Q4
Cites a GW150914 paper only for "a GW background has been searched for [84]"cited
The event appears only in titles of cited papers, even ones about GW resultscited
Names the event only as the occasion for something else, such as the telescopes that followed it upcited

For mixed cases, answer each question on its own. If a paper quotes "~1.4 M☉" in the introduction and uses the samples later, that's Q2. The rounded value adds nothing once a data product is used.

Two things to watch

  • Reference-list mentions: the event is in a cited paper's title. Judge from where the paper cites that reference, not from the title.
  • Counterpart names (GRB 170817A, AT2017gfo, SSS17a) name the electromagnetic counterpart, not the GW signal. The questions are about the GW results. Discussing or interpreting the counterpart is Q4. Only taking counterpart data as an input (e.g. plotting the kilonova light curve) is cited.

How sure are you?

  • clear: the paper plainly supports your answer.
  • probably: your best reading, but you might be wrong.
  • ambiguous: the text genuinely supports two answers under these rules (not just a hard paper). Say which question, if you can.

Not sure what the paper does at all, or outside your field? Skip it with a reason; that's better than a guess.
